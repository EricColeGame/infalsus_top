## Why the In Falsus Gacha Question Dominated the Conversation

Lowiro earned its reputation with Arcaea, a mobile rhythm game whose players understand exactly how free-to-play music games tend to monetize. So when the studio's second In Falsus trailer introduced cards, crafting, and something called Blueprints, that audience reacted the way any seasoned player would: genuine excitement about the charts, followed almost immediately by suspicion about the economy.

Community reports from rhythm game forums and the Arcaea Discord followed a familiar arc. Players praised the look of the note charts, then started asking whether the card layer was a soft-launch gacha in disguise. Card systems in live-service games usually signal one of two things — a long grind or a slot machine — and neither is appealing when all you want to do is hit notes on beat.

Director Guy addressed it head-on. In a brief message posted on the Arcaea Discord server, he stated plainly that there is no gacha. For a community that had already written several worst-case scenarios, that short line did a remarkable amount of work.

## How Cards, Crafting, and Blueprints Replace In Falsus Gacha

So if there's no In Falsus gacha, what fills the space where a pull system would normally sit? Based on the Steam store description and the Tokyo Game Show trailer, the answer is a crafting economy with a spatial puzzle on top.

Players collect materials, craft those materials into cards, and then slot the cards into Blueprints. Lowiro describes Blueprints as grids offering a wide variety of stat boosts and bonuses, and the store page language suggests arrangement genuinely matters — how you place materials within a Blueprint shapes the effects you end up with.

That last detail is the interesting one. A crafting system is deterministic: you can see what you're building toward and plan around it. A Blueprint grid adds a deck-building layer, because the same handful of cards can produce very different outcomes depending on layout. In other words, the skill expression isn't only in your fingers.

**Table 2: The systems replacing In Falsus gacha**

| System | What it does | What it means for you |
|---|---|---|
| Material collecting | Gathers resources through play | Progression comes from time, not pulls |
| Card crafting | Converts materials into cards | Deterministic building instead of RNG |
| Blueprints | Grids that hold your cards | Layout changes the bonuses you receive |
| Stat boosts and bonuses | Modifies performance | Likely higher scores and extra life-bar cushioning |

### Why "No Gacha" Doesn't Automatically Mean "No Grind"

It's worth being honest here: removing the In Falsus gacha removes randomness, not effort. Crafting systems can still be slow, and if materials are scarce, the grind itself becomes the pressure point that pushes players toward spending. That's a design choice, not a scam — but it's a real one.

The difference is transparency. With a pull system, you can spend money and walk away with nothing useful. With crafting, every material you gather moves you measurably closer to a card you actually want. Whether that trade feels generous depends entirely on drop rates and recipe costs, and neither has been published yet.

### What We Still Don't Know About Monetization

No pricing, no bundles, and no season pass structure have been announced. That's normal this far out from release, but it means "no gacha" should be read as a design commitment rather than a complete monetization roadmap. A game can skip gacha entirely and still sell song packs, cosmetic sets, or expansion content — all of which are standard in the rhythm genre.

## Rhythm Mechanics: Lanes, Flicks, and Keyboard-and-Mouse Precision

The gameplay footage shown at Tokyo Game Show points to a dual-input setup: keyboard and mouse working together, four central lanes, two side lanes, and flick-style aerial notes that float above the main playfield. If you've put hours into Osu-style games, the pointer-driven shape will feel familiar. If you grew up in arcades, the lane layout and flick notes will echo the feel of cabinets like Sound Voltex and Ongeki.

That combination is unusual for a PC-first rhythm game, and it's a big part of why the project has generated so much chatter. Most PC rhythm titles pick a single input philosophy and commit to it. In Falsus appears to want the precision of a mouse alongside the finger independence of a keyboard.

## The Visual Novel Half: Five Girls, One Intertwined Story

The other half of the package is a full visual novel, and Lowiro is not treating it as a side dish. The studio has confirmed five high school girls whose storylines interlock: Nia Cana, Mei Alder, Ayame Takatsuki, Iris Leal, and Story Étienne.

Lowiro promises full Japanese voice acting and describes the narrative as integral rather than decorative, with music gameplay, sound design, and themes all reflecting and directly linking to events in the story. That's an ambitious pitch. Rhythm games often bolt a story mode onto the side of the note charts; here, the claim is that the two halves are inseparable.

## Frequently Asked Questions About In Falsus Gacha

No. The game's director stated publicly on the Arcaea Discord server that there is no gacha. Cards are crafted from collected materials rather than pulled from a randomized banner.

You gather materials through play, craft them into cards, and arrange those cards inside Blueprints — grids that provide stat boosts and bonuses. Where you place a card inside a Blueprint affects the outcome, so the system rewards planning rather than luck.

Only a 2026 window has been announced, with no specific date. The game is confirmed for PC via Steam, and a five-song demo was playable at Tokyo Game Show.
